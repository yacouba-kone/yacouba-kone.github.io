Zero Velocity Detection without Motion
Pre-classiﬁcation: Uniform AI Model for All
pedestrian Motions (UMAM)
Yacouba Kone, Ni Zhu, Valerie Renaudin
AME-GEOLOC, Univ Gustave Eiffel, IFSTTAR
F-44344 Bouguenais, France
valerie.renaudin@univ-eiffel.fr
Abstract—Foot-mounted positioning devices are becoming
more and more popular in the different application ﬁeld. For
example, inertial sensors are now embedded in safety shoes to
monitor security. They allow positioning with zero velocity update
to bound the error growth of foot-mounted inertial sensors. High
positioning accuracy depends on robust zero velocity detector
(ZVD). Existing Artiﬁcial Intelligent (AI)-based methods classify
the pedestrian dynamics to adjust ZVD at the cost of high
computation costs and error propagation from miss-classiﬁcation.
We propose a machine learning model to detect zero velocity
moments without any pre-classiﬁcation step, named Uniform
AI Model for All pedestrian Motions (UMAM). Performance
is evaluated by benchmarking on two new subjects of opposite
gender and different size, not included in the training data set,
over complex indoor/outdoor paths of 2 km for subject 1 and 2.1
km for subject 2. We obtain an average 2D loop closure error
of less than 0.37%.
Index Terms —pedestrian navigation, inertial sensors, IMU,
machine learning, zero-velocity detection
I. I NTRODUCTION
The sensors embedded in the connected objects enable the
measurement of many physiological, mechanical and environ-
mental data of our daily life. For several years, the industry
has been working on integrating sensors into our textiles to
improve our health and mobility [1] or in smart devices to
monitor human activity [2]. Shoes with inertial sensors have
now been commercialized for professional applications, e.g.
to initiate the emergency response in the event of an accident.
The adoption of footwear with inertial sensors is still limited
to a few applications, especially sports, but greater adoption
is very likely to happen with an increasing offer of footwear.
With these foot sensors, it is now possible to compute
accurate indoor/outdoor travel tracks. Positioning algorithms
are mainly based on strap-down mechanization coupled with
the detection of zero velocity periods of the foot to calibrate
the inertial sensors errors. Despite very good performances,
considering the diversity of human walking gaits remains a
challenge. Consequently, research on adaptive zero-velocity
detection (ZVD) of the foot is still an active research topic.
Existing algorithms are ﬁrst classifying the different dynam-
ics (walking, running, side-stepping, etc.) to adapt the ZVD
part to the dynamical changes and the individual mobility
proﬁles [3], [4]. The successful classiﬁcation of the motion
modes and detection of the zero velocity periods depends on
two different time scales based on signal processing methods.
Whereas motion mode classiﬁcation is observed over several
seconds to recognize robustly the repetitive patterns of motion
(walking, running, stairs), ZVD depends on features estimated
over very small time sliding windows (4 msec for the running
case). As the movement speed increases, the period of contact
of the foot with the ground decreases. This phenomenon
explains why ZVD algorithms are split into two steps.
In this paper, a novel one-step machine learning-based
ZVD without separate pedestrian motion classiﬁcation is pro-
posed. It is UMAM-ZVD standing for a Uniform artiﬁcial
intelligence-based Model for All pedestrian Motions ZVD.
This strategy was sought for the following reasons. On one
hand, it reduces the computation costs, which eases the
implementation of real-time ZVD algorithms and therefore
real-time positioning algorithms. On the other hand, it avoids
cascading errors inherited from the miss-classiﬁcation of the
motion mode. Finally it eliminates the need for tuning the
models to human gait activity levels.
After a review of ZVD methods for pedestrian navigation
with an inertial measurement unit (IMU) attached to the foot,
the training data and UMAM are presented. The performance
is evaluated with two subjects, who did not participate in the
construction of the learning database, over a 2 km and a 2.1 km
long tracks. These are complex indoor and outdoor scenarios
involving walking, running, climbing, and descending stairs
without a break between movements. The performances of
UMAM are assessed at the positioning and ZUPT detection
levels comparing with three state-of-the-art methods.
II. S TATE OF THE ART
Zero-velocity UPdaTe (ZUPT) is a common technique used
to reduce the error drift for the foot-mounted inertial naviga-
tion systems. By introducing ZUPT as a pseudo-measurement
into the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) to correct the estimated
velocity after each stride, the cubic-in-time error growth of the
inertial sensors could be effectively mitigated. The research
regarding the stance phase detecting, i.e., ZVD, has been
conducted for more than one decade. The detection methods
have evolved towards a more and more robust way.
The ﬁrst generation of ZVDs are mostly based on the
signal energy and variance [5]–[8]. The main disadvantage
--- p2 ---
of these traditional ZVDs is the lack of robustness when
dealing with new subjects and motions in different dynamic.
The root cause for this constraint is their dependency on
the thresholds. These traditional energy-based ZVDs can be
reformulated as a generalized likelihood-ration (GLR) test,
by giving different prior knowledge of sensors. Consequently,
the same concern about threshold tuning exists for the GLR-
based ZVD, including the famous Stance Hypothesis Optimal
Detector (SHOE) [9].
Then, more and more research interest focus on the con-
struction of adaptive thresholds for different motions based
on the traditional ZVDs. The adaptive thresholds can be
designed based on the SHOE-ZVD with the help of extra
sensors such as pressure sensors [10], dynamic vision sen-
sor [11] or down-facing ultrasonic sensor. By involving the
measurements of theses extra sensors into the test statistic
of the GLR framework, the sensibility of the test statistic to
different motion dynamic could be improved which lead to
the adaptive thresholding. For these adaptative thresholding
methods, a compromise should be found between the cost,
volume of the extra sensors and the performance gain. In
addition, the threshold adapters could be realized by estab-
lishing a function or map between the motion type or other
variables and the targeted threshold. This approach often
needs a complementary motion classiﬁer [12] or gait cycle
segmentation layer [13] to better match the threshold function
or mapping. [13] proposed an adaptive thresholding based on
gait signal analysis and step detection. A temporal threshold
is ﬁrstly determined to distinguish two general motion types:
modest gait or fast gait. According to the motion type, this
proposed robust step detector is then conducted with an anti-
fault peaks and valley detection. Besides, the adaptive ZVD
can also be formulated into a Bayesian approach by modeling
a time-variant prior hypothesis and loss function [14], [15].
For the last two approaches of adaptive thresholding methods
previously mentioned, there are still a part of parameters to be
tuned depending on the hardware used even they are declared
to be not very sensitive.
Recently, the data-driven ZVDs are becoming more and
more popular thanks to their potential robustness for highly
varying motion types and subjects. But preliminary work is
needed such as data collection, data processing and labelling.
Most existing algorithms can be divided into two parts: a
motion classiﬁcation module and a stance phase detection
module. They can be both or partly realized by AI methods.
An SVM classiﬁer is applied by [12] to classify six motion
types. Then, the traditional SHOE [9] detector is associated
by using the pre-ﬁxed optimal thresholds for each motion.
Walking, running and a combined walking/running are tested
with the proposed algorithm. The results are proved to be
satisfactory. [4], [16] proposed to use a long short term
memory (LSTM) neural network to directly detect the stance
phase of a gait cycle without prior motion classiﬁcation and the
threshold tuning, which is a breakthrough among the ZVDs.
In our previous research work [3], two machine learning-based
ZVDs are proposed: Random Forest and Histogram-based

Machine Learning-Based Zero-Velocity
Detection for Inertial Pedestrian Navigation
Y acouba Kone, Ni Zhu , Valerie Renaudin , Member, IEEE, and Miguel Ortiz
Abstract —Zero velocity update is a common and efﬁcient
approach to bound the accumulated error growth for foot-
mounted inertial navigation system. Thus a robust zero veloc-
ity detector (ZVD) for all kinds of locomotion is needed for
high accuracy pedestrian navigation systems. In this paper,
we investigate two machine learning-based ZVDs: Histogram-
based Gradient Boosting (HGB) and Random Forest (RF),
aiming at adapting to different motion types while reduc-
ing the computation costs compared to the deep learning-
based detectors. A complete data pre-processing procedure,
including a feature engineering study and data augmentation
techniques, is proposed. A motion classiﬁer based on HGB
is used to distinguish “single support” and “double ﬂoat”
motions. This concept is different from the traditional locomotion classiﬁcation (walking, running, stair climbing) since
it merges similar motions into the same class. The proposed ZVDs are evaluated with inertial data collected by two
subjects over a 1.8 km indoor/outdoor path with different motions and speeds. The results show that without huge training
dataset, these two machine learning-based ZVDs achieve be tter performances (55 cm positioning accuracy) and lower
computational costs than the two deep learning-based LongShort-Term Memory methods (1.21 m positioning accuracy).
Index Terms— Pedestrian navigation, inertial sensors, IMU, machine learning, zero-velocity detection.

II. S T A TE OF THE ART
A. Existing Classic Zero-Velocity Detection Methods
Zero-velocity detectors (ZVDs) ﬁnd the instants that the
IMU sensors are static corresponding to the stance phase of a
walking gait. By detecting the ZUPT, the accumulated errors of
foot-mounted inertial sensors can efﬁciently be bounded. In the
current literature, most ZVDs use signal energy (e.g. from
acceleration and angular rate) and their variances to distinguish
the zero velocity instants. These kinds of detectors include:
• Acceleration or Angular Rate Moving Variance [14] or
Moving Average [15] Detector;
• Acceleration Magnitude Detector [16];
• Angular Rate Energy Detector [17];
• The Stance Hypothesis Optimal Detector (SHOE) [6]: this
detector is derived within a General Likelihood Ratio Test
(GLRT) framework, in which a test statistic is compared
with a predeﬁned threshold to make the decision.
Among the above detectors, the moving variance detector
can be combined with the magnitude or energy detector to
obtain a better performance [18]. However, the limitation
of these traditional detectors is their dependence on ﬁxed
thresholds. These thresholds vary signiﬁcantly according to
motion types (e.g. walking, running, climbing stairs, etc) as
well as individuals. It is not possible to ﬁnd a universal
threshold value for all kinds of motions neither all the subjects.
Therefore much research work about adaptive zero-velocity
detection methods and some threshold-less ZVD emerged in
the past years.
Usually, the adaptive ZVD is realized by establishing the
relationship between the user motion and the optimal thresh-
old. These approaches need abundant data collection as well
as high computational cost. Based on the traditional SHOE
detector, [19] proposed an adaptive ZVD by combining a pres-
sure sensor with accelerometer and gyroscope. It introduced a
new test statistic to add pressure sensor records. By enlarging
the difference of the test statistics between motion instants
and zero velocity instants, it can effectively mitigate the false
alarm detection. Then, by using the angular rate magnitude,
motion classiﬁcations are realized. Finally, two threshold func-
tions for walking/running and ascending/descending stairs are
established based on second-order polynomial ﬁtting. These
functions are applied accordin g to the motion classiﬁcation
results. A limitation of this adaptive method is its dependency
on extra sensors (pressure sensor). Although this sensor could
help improve the detection accu racy performance, at the same
time, it increases the complexity, the cost and the sensitivity
of the complete system. This would make the system less
ubiquitous, which limits the user case in some extreme envi-
ronments such as ﬁre-ﬁghters. Another limitation is linked
to the adaptive function established that is speciﬁc for one
subject. Thus the computation cost will increase when applied
to several subjects.
Reference [5] proposed an adaptive ZVD solely based on
inertial signals. It distinguishes the swing and the stance
phases of a gait cycle with a clustering algorithm. It partitions
the potential gait phases into true and false cluster thereby
yielding an efﬁcient elimination of the false gait phase. The
threshold is adapted to these different recognized gait cycle
stages but the corresponding parameters for each gait phase
still need to be tuned.
Different from the previous ZVDs which depend on prede-
ﬁned thresholds, [10] proposes a threshold-less ZVD. Based
on the shape of the simpliﬁed sensor signals through signal
processing procedures, it ﬁxes ﬁrst the most likely zero
velocity interval by detecting consecutive two positive pics
of angular rates. Then, the zero velocity instants are ﬁxed
within the previous interval by using a synthesized signal
constructed from the accelerometer signals. It is shown that
this algorithm can effectively detect the zero velocity moments
for different pedestrians but only the motion types of walking,
side-walking and backward walking at different speed are
tested and validated in [10]. I ts performance and robustness
for other motion types are still unknown.
B. Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI)-Based Zero Velocity
Detection Methods
A promising way to solve the ZVD problem for different
motion types is using Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI)-based meth-
ods. Some research work has already been conducted on ZVD
using AI approaches showing enc ouraging results. Generally,
AI-based approaches need a variety of data collected from dif-
ferent subjects and different motion types. These data should
be labeled to train the model in a supervised way. Existing
research work performs the data labelling with extra sensors
(e.g., pressure sensor [6]), manual annotation according to
certain criteria [20], or with the help of a high accuracy
motion capture system [13]. Then the training and validation
procedures is done either by machine learning or deep learning
approach. Most existing IA-based ZVDs can be divided into
two parts: a motion classiﬁcation module and a stance phase
detection module. They can be both or partly realized by
IA methods.

is ﬁrstly realized by SVM, where 24 motion types are involved
and the classiﬁcation can reach an accuracy of 98.27%.

Abstract—Robustly detecting steps with inertial sensors em-
bedded in smartphones remains a challenging problem for
pedestrian navigation, mainly due to the diversity of human
gaits. In this paper, we propose a new step detection method
for handheld devices, smartSTEP, that processes acceleration
and gyroscope signals with machine learning techniques. The
advantage of smartSTEP is that it does not rely on hand motion
mode classiﬁers nor thresholds calibration. Trained on 9000 steps
from 12 different participants and tested on approximately 2200
steps recorded on persons mostly not involved in the training, it
achieved 99% recall and 98.9% precision in challenging scenarios
such as asymmetrical walking, outdoor walking on different
surfaces with different hand motion modes, and stairs climbing. A
0.097 seconds root mean square error is achieved on the predicted
stride duration. This competes with the performances of present
algorithms dedicated to calculating stride duration.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Within the last few years, the demand for indoor naviga-
tion aids has been growing to increase human accessibility,
mobility, and autonomy. As wearable sensors are becoming
more and more ubiquitous in today’s world, so is their ap-
plication in indoor navigation. Almost all wearable devices
and smartphones are embedded with Inertial Measurement
Units (IMU) whose interest increased because of their low
cost, low power consumption, and lightweight nature. Methods
and systems supporting inertial signals and indoor navigation
encompass speciﬁc information about human gait to increase
their accuracy.
One of the most promising methods is Pedestrian Dead
Reckoning (PDR). PDR is a localization technique mostly
used by smartphones processing IMU data to calculate users’
location. It is more popular than other methods because it
is self-contained, simple, and does not require the existence
of a dedicated and expensive infrastructure of beacons. It
exploits information from gait kinematics to detect step in-
stants, estimate step length, and calculate walking direction.
The successive position is propagated recursively based on the
previous position estimate and the step length and walking
direction estimates. Nevertheless, it raises many scientiﬁc
challenges when applied on handheld units because of the
complex nature of the user’s hand motion. A reason for this
complexity is that handheld devices measure movements that
are not related to global locomotion. Upper and lower body
movements can be decorrelated.
A key player in the robustness, accuracy, and scalability
of PDR algorithms is the step detection algorithm. Despite
decades of research, the problem of step detection from
handheld IMU is still challenging. The performance of present
methods is inﬂuenced by setting correct thresholds, which can
be different for various physiological and dynamic parameters.
Correctly identifying motion modes of hand also plays an
important role in the performance. However, the motion mode
of the hand (texting/swinging hand or irregular motion) can
be ambiguous especially at slow walking speeds.
In this paper, we propose a new method to tackle existing
challenges on step detection. The adopted technique relies on
machine learning models to detect steps irrespective of the
hand motion mode and whatever the dynamics and physiology
of the user are. This technique surpasses the above-mentioned
problems by learning empirical features that correspond to
steps in whatever gait style. The method is driven by human
gait learning. It learns cyclic instants that correspond to steps
on human gait IMU signals collected by handheld wearables.
As cyclic instants are also present while climbing stairs, we
tend also to solve step detection in staircase scenarios. It is
called smartSTEP for SMARTphone inertial sensors based
STEP detection driven by human gait learning. Unlike deep
learning techniques, smartSTEP utilize interpretable physical
features such like the walking frequency to detect steps. This
was the main interest of using machine learning models.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2
describes the state-of-the-art of handheld IMU-based step
detection techniques. Section 3 presents the training dataset
used to build the new step detection approach, which is
detailed in Section 4. Section 5 is dedicated to the performance
assessment of the proposed step detection method. Finally, the
conclusion is drawn in Section 6.

DETECTION TECHNIQUES
Step detection is a crucial issue in PDR since it can lead
to substantial errors due to under-counting steps and false
detecting them. It remains marginally explored for handheld
devices whereas it is largely addressed with ”body ﬁxed”
sensors (at the pelvis or the on the foot). Existing techniques
can be categorized into two main groups:
• Methods based on the detection of cyclic events (peak,
valley, zero-crossing). [1]–[7],
• Methods implementing artiﬁcial intelligence. [8], [9].
A. Methods based on cyclic event detection
A large proportion of step detection methods is based on
peak/valley detection. Peak detection deﬁnes steps in line with
the appearance of periodic peaks in the IMU signals. Although
the advantage of such a method is simplicity, it is limited to
certain walking manners. The main differences found between
existing peak detection methods are the pre-processing, the
setting of adaptive peak characteristics or thresholds, and
ﬁnally the post-processing or validation of detected steps.
Types of pre-processing involve gait and hand motion mode
(swinging-texting-irregular) recognition. Hand motion mode
classiﬁcation can be threshold-based or machine learning-
based. Susi et al. [2] proposed a threshold-based hand motion
mode classiﬁer before applying step detection on the IMU
signals. A decision of whether to use peak detection on the
acceleration norm or valley detection on the angular velocity
norm is based on the classiﬁed hand motion mode. Despite the
accuracy of the proposed classiﬁer, this study does not account
for step detector errors in case of misclassiﬁcation. Trong
Bui et al. [4] proposed two machine learning-based motion
mode classiﬁer and a step detector relying on setting speciﬁc
peak and valley thresholds for each classiﬁed motion mode.
To aid magnify peaks that correspond to step events, adding
peak-based adaptive thresholds has been suggested. Using that,
they achieved an accuracy of 98%. However, measuring just
accuracy is misleading as it involves the correct prediction
of step and nonstep events in the gait cycle. As nonstep
instants are much greater than step events, high accuracy
cannot quantify the wrong detections. Lu et al. [6] detected
steps as a pair of peak-valley using some peak-valley dynamic
related characteristics. They achieved a 0.03% error in step
count. However, step count is not enough to pinpoint mis-
detected and over-detected steps. In their experimental testing
conditions, they achieved only 1.17% improved accuracy over
the classical peak detection. Khedr et al. [5] also used adaptive
thresholding on acceleration norm to achieve a 99.6% on
counting steps during continuous walking. Lee et al. [10]
proposed peak characteristics based on window calculated
statistics. Although such methods provide accurate character-
istics for peak detection, these calculated characteristics might
fail to detect peaks when motion mode changes abruptly.
Finally, validation of detected steps is done through magnitude
and temporal ﬁltering. Peaks are validated against speciﬁed
signal magnitude thresholds, or a speciﬁed temporal peak
separation, which is based on the walking frequency [4]–[6].
B. Methods involving machine learning
Very few studies utilize machine learning approaches to
directly detect step instants. Kupke et al. [9] proposed a
feedforward artiﬁcial neural network on the z-axis acceler-
ation patterns for step detection and step length estimation.
However, the dataset used for the training is very small, 1000
steps, and no clear description of which activities or motion
modes of the hand are considered. Shao et al. [8] used deep
convolutional neural networks on acceleration signals for the
main purpose of creating a pedometer. However, their model
does not perform well in hand swinging mode with a true
positive rate of around 90% and no mention of a false positive
rate. Mainly, the purpose of machine learning models is limited
to hand motion mode classiﬁers and step length estimation.
They are used as side methods to reinforce peak detection
rather than being the main step detection techniques.
III. D ESCRIPTION OF THE DATASETS
A. Training dataset: Description of the scenarios
Twelve healthy participants with no gait problems (age
29 ± 8 years, height 1.7 ± 0.07 m, body mass 73 ± 11 kg,
3 F and 9 M) volunteered for the data collection. Eight of
them walked on an instrumented treadmill at three different
speeds (low-comfortable-high). The other four walked on the
treadmill while being operated in an asymmetrical mode,
which is enabled by operating each belt with a different speed
(one at 0.7m/s and one at 1m/s, Fig. 1a). Despite the fact
that treadmill walking has been associated with shorter stride
length, increased cadence, and reduced natural variability,
the characteristics, peaks and valleys of the IMU signals
still exist due to the periodic nature of gait [11]. Moreover,
kinematic and kinetic patterns are similar between overground
walking and treadmill [12], [13]. In addition, one participant
recorded around 120 steps climbing and descending stairs. In
total around 9000 steps are present in the training dataset.
The dataset includes different hand motion modes: texting
and swinging. Finally, recordings from static and irregular
hand movements are also added to the dataset. Irregular hand
movements include shaking and waving.
B. Experimental setup
The smartphone dataset was recorded using the iPhone
6s with a commercial application called ”sensor play” [14].
The sampling frequency was set to 100Hz. Two instruments
were used to validate the labeling of step events and test
the proposed method’s performance. Indoors, the instrumented
treadmill was used as a reference (Fig. 1a). The instrumented
treadmill recorded ground reaction forces at 600 Hz. For the
climbing and descending stairs scenarios, the gaitup® foot-
mounted IMU units were used as reference [15] (Fig. 1b).
It includes an accelerometer ±8g and a gyroscope ±2000dps
that register data with a sampling frequency of 128 Hz. Unlike
optical systems, these devices can capture large number of
strides and get outdoor measurements.

Abstract—Data-driven methods have attracted the research
community from all sectors including positioning-based appli-
cations. However, the performances of the AI-based methods
depend strongly on the quality of the data. With the fast de-
velopment of powerful hardware, collecting, storing and training
huge databases are not problematic anymore. The true bottleneck
to AI is rather getting high-quality labeling of the data, especially
for supervised learning. This paper aims at discussing the most
suitable and efficient way to label the step instants of wearables,
between the choices of using physical approaches and the pattern
interpretation approach. Physical approaches refer to using
highly accurate foot-mounted equipment to get the step instants
then project them on the related body parts. While the pattern
interpretation approach relies directly on the signal signatures
interpreted with the help of human gait knowledge. It is referred
to as the ”brain” approach. Two machine learning-based step
prediction models are trained with respectively the ”physic” and
”brain” labeling approach. The performance assessment shows
that the step prediction model trained with brain labeling has a
true positive detection rate around 85.9% - 95.7% with almost no
overdetection while the model trained with physical labeling can
only reach 54.7% of true positive rate with a high overdetection
rate (around 36.7%).
I. I NTRODUCTION
Inertial sensors embedded in wearable devices can measure
human movements. The processing of inertial signals targets
various objectives: better health, crowd mobility observation
or navigation in complex environments. A critical element
common to the success of many of these applications is the
accuracy of step counting. Steps must only be detected when
the person is moving and not in static periods even with
upper body part movements (e.g. beating eggs). The pedometer
is a popular step counting step device, whose accuracy is
questionable. For example, the study [1] gives a step counting
error between -22.7% and -1.5% for the wearable devices for
two 500 and 1500 steps treadmill trials conducted with 14
participants wearing 6 sensors each.
To overcome this difficulty, recent motion tracking methods
are increasingly based on artificial intelligence (AI). The latter
holds the promise of greater reliability in complex situations
where conventional signal processing methods struggle. To be
efficient, these methods require data and precise knowledge
of the phenomena to be learned. Defining a method to fix
high-quality labels in the inertial signals remains challenging.
Fig. 1: Human gait cycle [2]
Physics defines a step as the foot being flat on the ground
in the human gait cycle (Fig. 1). It seems logical to use this
instant to annotate inertial data. But will these instants always
be projected to epochs in the wearable signals patterns that
are relevant for learning? Is another approach that interprets
the patterns directly on the signal signatures using human
gait knowledge more meaningful? This paper conducts this
analysis. It adopts the two labeling approaches to learn two
distinct optimised models whose performance are tested on 3
indoor/outdoor ambulatory trials, including 2 healthy persons,
1 blind person and 3 ways to wear the sensor.
II. R ELATED RESEARCH WORK
A. State-of-the-art technologies of labeling step instants
For the foot-mounted devices, the step instant detection is
generally based on Zero Velocity Detection. To get the footstep
targets, the most traditional technique is using a motion capture
system or other complementary sensors (such as pressure
sensors [3] or dynamic vision sensor [4]). For the hand-held
devices, however, the step instances are not as obvious as the
foot-mounted ones since the steps occur physically at the foot
level and the regular or random movements of the hands can
strongly disturb the gait patterns. This is the reason why two
branches of labeling methods exist for the hand-held devices.
The first category is to indirectly use the step instance detected
by the foot-mounted devices possibly aided with other highly
accurate external systems. Then these step instances based on
the foot-mounted devices will be projected onto the hand-held
signals. The second category is directly based on the signal
gait patterns of the hand-held devices using traditional signal
processing techniques such as the Zero-Cross Detection (ZCD)

B. Background research work: SMARTstep
SmartSTEP [6] was developed to robustly detect step in-
stants for all types of human gait with inertial sensors located
on different user’s body parts (handheld, trouser pocket, hand
bag, etc.). The interpretation of the cyclic patterns of the
inertial data, recorded on several locations of the person’s
body, identified the most relevant epochs corresponding to
the labels for the AI learning phase. They do not necessarily
correspond to the flat foot periods but are stable labels in
repetitive patterns, regardless of the location of the sensor on
the body. Robustness is achieved by exploiting two machine-
learned models, which run in parallel on acceleration and
gyroscope data. A logical decision is made to switch between
the prediction of each model when one of the two models is
no longer effective. Static phases are included in the learning
process. The inertial data labeling has been done as a semi-
supervised task that requires human intervention to relocate
the labels potentially misplaced on the patterns. This approach
is referred to as ”brain” labeling in the rest of the paper as
opposed to ”physics” labeling. It achieved a 99% recall and
precision in challenging conditions with young adults’ and
blind persons. More details about smartSTEP is given in [6].

B. ”Physics” based labeling
The ”Physics” based approach annotates the step instant
frames using the foot-mounted sensor records processed by
UMAM [8]. It is an AI-based algorithm that detects steps using
the ZUPT (Zero velocity UpdaTe) epochs. A step is detected
when the foot is entirely on the ground and stationary. During
this short period (about 0.3s), the accelerometer only senses
the gravity resulting in a constant 9.81m/s² foot acceleration
magnitude. UMAM labels the step instant by the first frame
of the plateau. Between 2 steps detected on the same foot, the
other foot is making a step. This step instant is labeled at the
midpoint of 2 stride steps. Figure 3 shows the labeling result

rate (middle) magnitude of the IMU held in hand.

depending on the wearable’s attachment point. Figure 4 shows

A Survey on Artificial Intelligence for Pedestrian
Navigation with Wearable Inertial Sensors
Hanyuan Fu, Yacouba Kone, Valerie Renaudin, Ni Zhu
Univ. Gustave Eiffel, AME-GEOLOC
F-44344 Bouguenais, France

Abstract—Miniaturized IMU (inertial measurement units) are
widely integrated in wearable devices, promoting the versatile
and low cost pedestrian inertial navigation technology, especially
for indoor environment. In recent years, AI (Artificial Intelli-
gence) is applied to improve the performance of this technology.
AI methods work with data samples, thus it is important to select
a suitable process for segmenting the inertial data sequences. This
survey classifies AI methods for pedestrian inertial navigation
into two categories, namely human gait driven methods and
sampling frequency driven methods, according to their data
segmentation process. Human gait driven methods segment the
inertial measurement sequence by gait (step or stride) events
and learn to infer a gait vector (step/stride length and direction)
given a gait segment. Sampling frequency driven methods learn
to infer the user’s velocity or change in position given a fixed-
length segment of inertial measurements. The survey studies the
underlying assumptions and their validity of the two categories
of AI methods. Two methods (SELDA and RoNIN), each from
a category, are chosen for evaluation and comparison, on three
testing tracks totaling 770m, covering indoor and outdoor en-
vironment, including stairs. The experiments highlight the two
methods’ advantages and limitations, supporting the theoretical
analyses. The selected methods achieve 7m and 12m positioning
errors, respectively.
Index Terms —Indoor positioning, inertial sensors, pedestrian
navigation, dead reckoning, Machine Learning, Deep Learning
I. I NTRODUCTION
Pedestrian positioning technology with inertial sensors is
used in many daily life applications such as navigation apps
and Augmented Reality games. This choice results from the
lack of GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) signals
indoors and degraded performance of GNSS positioning even
in urban canyons. Indoor positioning technologies can also
be based on radio beacon networks deployed in buildings.
But this technology faces many problems such as high layout
cost, changing indoor radio propagation context, crowd effect,
human body tissue absorption, and so on. Inertial naviga-
tion systems have become more and more popular because
they are infrastructure-free solutions and low cost. But their
performance is degraded by inertial signals drift during a
long period, lack of robustness when dealing with variable
pedestrian motion modes (slow/normal/fast walking, going
up or down stairs, stationary, etc.), device poses (in swing-
ing hand, "texting", in pocket, etc.) and individual walking
characteristics. Many researchers apply Artificial Intelligence
(AI), namely traditional machine learning and deep learning to
mitigate these issues and improve the accuracy and robustness
of their models. In this context, this paper surveys AI-based
approaches for pedestrian navigation with wearable inertial
sensors. It shows that these approaches can be classified
into the 2 following classes depending on how the inertial
measurement data is aggregated to gather the most of useful
information.
1) Human gait driven AI methods: Inspired by the nature
of human walking, inertial signals are segmented by the user’s
gait (step or stride) events. Each segment can be utilized to
estimate the user’s step vector: length and direction.
2) Sampling Frequency driven AI methods: Inherited from
the acceleration double integration approach, inertial signals
are segmented into fixed length sequences, overlapping or not.
AI methods are trained to infer the user’s velocity or change
in position given a fixed length of inertial measurements. The
length of a segment partly depends on the measurement’s
sampling frequency.
This survey details the two-categories proposed to classify
existing AI based pedestrian navigation methods with wear-
able inertial sensors. It analyses their principal hypotheses,
advantages and limitations, both at theoretical and experi-
mental levels. Theoretical analysis identifies the assumptions
underlying the learning methods and reviews their validity.
Experimental assessment is conducted in each category with
one representative method selected in each category. The
experimental assessment is conducted on 3 testing tracks,
each is about 250 meters, including both indoor and outdoor
environment, stairs, with the user holding the device in front
of his chest.
Section II and III present the AI based pedestrian naviga-
tion state-of-the art methods: the human gait driven and the
sampling frequency driven AI methods, respectively. The two
methods selected in the aforementioned categories are detailed
in section IV. Section V is dedicated to the experimental as-
sessment, including the evaluation and comparison of the two
selected methods on pedestrian tracks. Section VI concludes
the survey.

on armband,...) is challenging, since the signals of different

the acceleration norm or a valley in the angular velocity

they propose 2 parallel Histogram Gradient Boosting Decision

process is designed to combine the output of the 2 models.
Tested for normal and visually impaired gaits, it achieves 97%

Once the IMU measurement signals are segmented by gait
events, each segment can be utilized to estimate the current
step/stride length and its direction. In the literature so far, step
length and direction are estimated separately.
A. A Two-step Estimation Process: step/stride length and
walking direction
1) Step or Stride length Estimation: Step length varies
from person to person and even for the same person. It
depends on the motion modes (normal/fast walking, going up
or down stairs). Even for the same person and same motion,
different device poses on the user’s body result in different
signal patterns. When the physical modeling becomes too
complicated, Artificial Intelligence comes in handy.
One common approach is to extract features manually
from a step/stride segment. Wang and al. [3] classify relevant
features into statistical (mean, standard deviation, maximum,
etc.), time-domain (number of peaks, zero-crossing ratio, etc.),
frequency domain (dominant frequencies, spectrum energy,
etc.) and higher-level features (based on empirical models).
The extracted features are utilized in an ensemble regression
model combining 6 regressors: Extreme Gradient Boost (XG-
Boost) [4], LightGBM [2], K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) [5],
Decision Tree (DT) [6], AdaBoost [7] and Support Vector
Regression (SVR) [8]. Klein and al. [9] leverage feature
selection to accelerate the deep learning process. They extract
60 candidate features from the input sequence, perform feature
selection with Monte-Carlo runs and ridge regression, then
the selected features are fed to a convolutional neural network
to regress step length. Inspired by the Weinberg Model [10],
Zhang et al. [11] express the acceleration in the global frame
via device attitude tracking, remove the gravity component,
filtered it with moving average and take the differences of
peak and valley of vertical acceleration as input features to an
OS-ELM (Online Sequential Extreme Learning Machine) [12]
to regress step length.
An alternative to feature engineering is end-to-end regres-
sion with the step or stride sequence. Gu et al. [13] use 2
stacked autoencoders and a dense layer to learn step length
from a step sequence. Yang et al. [14]’s step length model is
a Deep Believe Network (DBN) built with multiple Gaussian
Bernoulli Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBM, for feature
extraction) [15] and a regression layer on top. Wang et al. [16]
combine the stride sequence with other empirical features as
their model’s input. Their model is detailed in section IV.
2) Heading estimation: In earlier PDR frameworks, head-
ing is usually estimated by Extended Kalman Filter fusing
the device’s angular velocity and magnetometer readings,
assuming that the offset between the device’s and the user’s
pointing directions is approximately constant, which is the
case of "texting" or "calling" modes. This assumption is not
true in the "swinging" or "pocket" modes. More recently deep
learning methods are applied to infer the user’s heading from
sequences of the IMU measurements.
Zhang et al. [11] found recognizable patterns in the se-
quences of the device’s azimuth angle and magnetometer
readings when the user changes direction. Thus, they select
them as input features to their OS-ELM network to regress the
user’s heading angle. Wang et al. [17] proposes a hierarchical
LSTM [18] architecture associated with a spatial transformer
[19] to regress walking direction of each step made by a
pedestrian, taking as input 3D acceleration, angular rate and
magnetometer readings over a complete trajectory, leveraging
the strong correlation between the headings of the adjacent
steps.
B. Analysis of Underlying Assumptions
The main interest of this approach is that it’s based on
physical modeling of human gait. AI methods driven by
realistic gait changes are close to the physical facts and thus
more explainable. Gait driven AI methods are based on the
following hypotheses, proven or empirical.
1) Human walking is cyclic: In [20], walking locomotion
is described as a process in which the erect, moving body is
supported by first one leg, and then the other. As the moving
body passes over the supporting leg, the other leg swings
forward to prepare for its next supporting phase.
2) The gait cycles and the movements of the upper and
lower body limbs’ movements are correlated: It allows gait
event detection with wearable sensors. According to [21], dur-
ing normal walking, head and trunk travel as a unit and move
“up and down” as the center of gravity follows the mechanics
of the limbs. During each stride, the arms reciprocally flex and
extent.
3) Human paces are regular and constrained: According
to a statistical study presented by [3], within their dataset
containing 13.5km and 10145 strides of gait measurements
collected by a foot mounted device, including fast, normal
and slow walking, 99.5% of strides were within 1.55m and
no stride exceeds 1.75m. The mean and standard deviation of
stride length are 1.33m and 0.18m.
4) Step/stride length and inertial signals collected from
different body parts are correlated: Empirical models are de-
veloped based on foot mounted sensors, for example Kim [22]
found correlation between mean acceleration norm and stride
length and Ladetto [23] found correlation between acceleration
